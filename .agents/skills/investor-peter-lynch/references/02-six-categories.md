# The Six Categories — What to Expect and When to Sell Each
> Source: *One Up on Wall Street* Ch. 7 "I've Got It, I've Got It — What Is It?" (classification), Ch. 17 "The Best Time to Buy and Sell" (sell rules per category), Ch. 15 "The Final Checklist" (per-category checklist); *Beating the Street* Ch. 8-17 (applied cases: cyclicals, S&Ls, Fannie Mae, restaurants, utilities). Distilled 2026-09-22.

## Core thesis
Before anything else, decide which of six categories the company belongs to. The category sets (a) the return you should expect, (b) the metric that matters most, (c) the holding period, and (d) the specific event that constitutes a sell. Most errors come from mismatched expectations: waiting for a tenbagger from a stalwart, or holding a cyclical through the down-cycle as if it were a fast grower. Companies migrate between categories over their lives (fast grower → stalwart → slow grower; cyclical → turnaround), so re-classify each year.

## The categories

| Category | Definition (Lynch) | Expect | Key metric | Sell when (story change) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Slow grower** | Large, aged company growing ~2-4%/yr, roughly GDP; usually pays a generous, regular dividend (utilities, mature industrials). | Dividend + little price gain. 30-50% over a few years is a good outcome. | Dividend yield, payout history, whether dividends were ever cut. | Growth has stalled two years in a row; dividend under threat; company begins diworsifying with the cash instead of paying it out; no new products; market share loss. |
| **Stalwart** | Big, established, growing earnings ~10-12%/yr (his 1989 examples: Coca-Cola, Bristol-Myers, P&G, Colgate, Kellogg). | 30-50% gain in 1-2 years, then rotate into another undervalued stalwart. Downside protection in recessions. | P/E vs its own history and vs peers; PEG; whether it's overpriced relative to its steady growth. | P/E runs well above its normal range; new products in the last two years mixed and the next batch untested; growth rate slowing; diworsification; you've made your 30-50% and a cheaper stalwart exists. |
| **Fast grower** | Small, aggressive, growing earnings 20-25%/yr; may be in a slow industry (the best kind). Source of tenbaggers. | 10x-40x if the story holds for years; also the highest risk (esp. if under-financed). | Earnings growth 20-25% (>25% suspicious), PEG, balance sheet, whether the formula is proven in more than one place and there is still room to expand. | Expansion is running out (few new places to open); same-store sales down; institutional ownership high and everyone recommends it; P/E out of line with growth (e.g. P/E 30 for 15-20% growth); top executives and key people leaving; the company is telling a story to the public more than executing. |
| **Cyclical** | Sales and profits rise and fall in a regular but not perfectly predictable way with the economy (autos, airlines, steel, chemicals, tires). | Big gains if you buy at the trough and sell near the peak; big losses if you buy when they look cheap on peak earnings. | Inventories and the supply/demand balance; timing in the cycle; a **low P/E on a cyclical is a warning** (peak earnings), a high P/E may mean trough. | End of the cycle: inventories building up and the company can't move them; commodity prices falling; capex for new capacity announced industry-wide; competition from new entrants/price cuts; the company doubling its expansion budget at the top. |
| **Turnaround** | Battered, depressed, often near bankruptcy or in a bad patch (Chrysler 1982, Penn Central). Sub-types: bail-out, "who-would-have-thunk-it", "little-problem-we-didn't-anticipate", restructuring-the-hidden-good-company. | Large, fast gains largely independent of the market if the recovery holds; total loss if it doesn't. | Can it survive the creditors (cash vs debt; debt structure — bank debt due on demand vs long-term)? Is the business coming back? Costs being cut? | The turnaround is complete and everyone knows it (re-classify, usually to cyclical or stalwart); debt rising again after having fallen; inventories rising 2x sales growth; P/E inflated relative to earnings prospects; the strongest division sells 50% of output to one customer who is slowing. |
| **Asset play** | Company sitting on something the Street missed: cash, real estate, subscriber base, tax-loss carryforwards, natural resources, a subsidiary worth more than the whole. | Depends on the asset being surfaced (sale, spinoff, activist, buyback); patience required. | Value of the asset vs market cap; debt that could eat the asset; insider/raider interest; institutional ownership (low = still hidden). | Shares issued to fund diworsification; asset sold for less than expected; the raider or the market has already priced the asset (it's no longer hidden); institutional ownership from ~25% to ~60%. |

## Specific claims, mechanisms & data (from the books)
- Ch. 7: he estimates that a stalwart bought at a fair price should be held for a 30-50% gain and then swapped — "I'm not expecting a tenbagger from Bristol-Myers".
- Ch. 7 and 17: fast growers with a 20-25% growth rate are the sweet spot; growth above 25% tends to be in hot industries and is hard to sustain (see `04-metrics-and-thresholds.md`).
- Ch. 7: cyclicals are where timing is everything and the inexperienced investor "can lose more than 50%" by buying near the peak because the P/E looks low.
- *Beating the Street* Ch. 16 "Nuts and Bolts of Cyclicals" (Phelps Dodge, autos): the time to buy is when the news is bad, inventories are falling, and the P/E is high on depressed earnings.
- Turnarounds: Chrysler 1982 (over $1B in cash, so it would survive) is his canonical example (*One Up*, Ch. 7 and Ch. 17; *Beating the Street*, Ch. 15 on autos).

## How to APPLY (decision rules)
1. Pick **one** primary category; note a secondary if it is transitioning (e.g. "stalwart drifting to slow grower"). State the evidence (growth rate, size, dividend, cyclicality, balance sheet).
2. Write the expected return and holding period appropriate to the category; refuse to promise fast-grower returns from a stalwart.
3. Pull the category-specific sell trigger from the table and write it down at purchase — that is the "story change" you will test each quarter.
4. For cyclicals, invert the P/E logic: low P/E near peak earnings is dangerous; do not compute PEG naively.
5. For turnarounds, the first gate is survival: cash vs debt, and the *type* of debt (bank/short vs funded/long).
6. Re-classify at least annually and whenever the growth rate crosses ~10% or ~20%.

## Caveats / where he hedges
- The categories are his heuristic, not a taxonomy with hard edges; many companies straddle two (he says so, Ch. 7).
- The 10-12% stalwart / 20-25% fast grower bands are 1989 nominal figures in a higher-inflation era; apply the same *logic*, not necessarily the same nominal cutoffs.
- Asset plays require patience and a catalyst; he warns that hidden assets can stay hidden and that management can squander them.
