# Stocks I'd Avoid — Lynch's Red Flags
> Source: *One Up on Wall Street* Ch. 9 "Stocks I'd Avoid" (hottest stock in the hottest industry, "the next" something, diworsification, whisper stocks, the middleman/one-customer company, the stock with the exciting name), Ch. 18 "The Twelve Silliest Things People Say About Stock Prices", Ch. 8 (perfect-stock attributes inverted); *Beating the Street* Ch. 1 (25 Golden Rules). Distilled 2026-09-22.

## Core thesis
Most avoidable losses come from buying stories rather than companies. The most dangerous stock is the one everybody agrees is wonderful, in the industry everybody agrees is the future, at a price that already assumes the future. Fast growth in a hot industry attracts competitors and capital, so the growth is rarely sustained; the P/E collapses even if the business is fine.

## The red flags (Ch. 9)
| Flag | What Lynch means | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Hottest stock in the hottest industry** | The one that gets the most publicity, that everyone hears about in the car pool; hot industries attract competition, and the stock falls as fast as it rose. | Is the industry the current consensus growth theme? Is the stock the consensus leader? Earnings growth >25%? P/E far above growth? |
| **"The next X"** | A company promoted as the next IBM / McDonald's / Intel almost never is; and when it is announced, it is often a sign the original is peaking too. | Is the bull case framed by analogy rather than by this company's own numbers? |
| **Diworsification** | Profitable companies that squander cash on foolish acquisitions outside their competence (his 1960s conglomerate and 1980s examples); the acquirer overpays and later writes it off. | Last 3 years of M&A: related or unrelated? Goodwill growing faster than revenue? Restructuring charges? |
| **Whisper stocks** | The long-shot with a great story and no earnings — the cure for cancer, the oil-from-nothing technology. "Buy it later, when it's proven." Waiting costs little; the first doubling after proof is still available. | Does the company have earnings? A proven formula? If the story is entirely future, wait. |
| **Dependent on one customer** | The supplier whose fortunes are decided by a single buyer (his example: component makers selling 25-50%+ to IBM); the customer has the pricing power and can cancel. | Customer concentration disclosed in the 10-K (any customer >10% of sales)? |
| **The stock with the exciting name** | The opposite of the boring-name rule; a flashy name attracts a crowd and a premium. | Cosmetic, but note it. |
| **Story-only stocks** | A stock whose case rests on a narrative that cannot yet be seen in earnings, cash, or unit growth. | Can each element of the story be tied to a line in the financials? |

## Things people say that Lynch calls silly (Ch. 18) — do not use as reasons
- "If it's gone down this much already, it can't go much lower." / "It's gone up this much, how can it go higher?"
- "It's only $3 a share, what can I lose?" (you lose 100% either way)
- "Eventually they always come back." (many never do)
- "It's always darkest before the dawn." / "When it rebounds to $10 I'll sell."
- "Conservative stocks don't fluctuate much." / "It's taking too long for anything to happen."
- "Look at all the money I've lost: I didn't buy it!" / "I missed that one, I'll catch the next one."
- "The stock's gone up, so I must be right; gone down, so I must be wrong."

## How to APPLY (decision rules)
1. Run the seven flags above and mark **HIT / NOT HIT / UNKNOWN** with a one-line reason each.
2. Any HIT on "whisper stock" (no earnings, story only) → Lynch's answer is *wait for proof*, regardless of upside.
3. HIT on "hottest in hottest" is not an automatic sell of an existing position, but it forces the P/E-vs-growth check and a written sell trigger.
4. Diworsification HIT → discount management; prefer buybacks/dividends as capital return evidence.
5. Never write a verdict reason that matches one of the "silly things" list.

## Caveats / where he hedges
- Some hot-industry leaders did keep compounding (he acknowledges missing many technology winners; he owned few tech names). The flag is about base rates and price, not a claim that leaders always fail.
- "One customer" concentration is normal in some industries (defense, semicap); weigh contract length and switching costs rather than treating it as binary.
- His whisper-stock rule ("wait for earnings") systematically misses early biotech/tech multi-baggers; that is the accepted trade-off of the lens.
