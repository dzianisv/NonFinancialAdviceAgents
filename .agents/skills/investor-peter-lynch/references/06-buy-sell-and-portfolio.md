# Buying, Selling, and Portfolio Construction — Story Change, Not Price; As Many as You Can Follow
> Source: *One Up on Wall Street* Ch. 16 "Designing a Portfolio", Ch. 17 "The Best Time to Buy and Sell", Ch. 18 (price fallacies), Ch. 19 "Options, Futures, and Shorts" (avoid), Ch. 20 "50,000 Frenchmen Can Be Wrong"; *Beating the Street* Ch. 1 "25 Golden Rules", Ch. 2 "The Weekend Worrier", Ch. 3-6 (Magellan years), Ch. 17-18 (Fannie Mae, rotation). Distilled 2026-09-22.

## Core thesis
You do not need to time the market; you need to own good companies and sell them when *their* story changes. The portfolio is organized by category so you know what each holding is for. Hold as many stocks as you can genuinely follow — for a part-time investor that is a handful — and keep rotating from names whose story has played out (or broken) into names where the story is intact and the price is better. Cash is acceptable when nothing meets the test, but it is not a market call.

## When to buy (Ch. 17)
- Any time the story is right and the price is reasonable — he does not wait for a market signal.
- Two recurring bargain windows he names: **year-end tax selling** (October-December) and **market collapses/corrections**, when good companies are sold indiscriminately. Buy the companies you already researched.
- Do not buy just because the stock is down; buy because the story is intact and the P/E vs growth is now attractive.

## When to sell — story change, per category (Ch. 17; see `02-six-categories.md` table)
- Slow grower: growth stalls, dividend at risk, diworsification.
- Stalwart: P/E stretched vs history; 30-50% gain achieved and a better stalwart is available; growth slows.
- Fast grower: expansion ending, same-store sales down, P/E out of line with growth, executives leaving, "everyone" owns it.
- Cyclical: inventories building, prices falling, capacity additions, end of the cycle.
- Turnaround: turnaround complete (reclassify); debt rising again; inventories 2x sales growth.
- Asset play: shares issued to diworsify; the asset is priced in; institutional ownership crowded.

## Never sell on price alone (Ch. 17-18; *Beating the Street* Golden Rules)
- Selling because the stock is up ("take profits") or down ("cut losses") without a story change is the classic amateur error; it is how you cut the flowers and water the weeds.
- A price drop with an intact story is a buying opportunity in the categories where the story is durable; a price rise with an intact story is not a reason to sell a fast grower — the tenbagger is the point.
- The exception is valuation: when the P/E is far above what the growth rate justifies, that *is* a story-related change (the price now assumes a story that is not true).

## Portfolio construction (Ch. 16)
- **Number of holdings**: "as many as you can follow" — not a fixed number. For the part-time investor he suggests owning **3 to 10** stocks is sensible; owning more names than you can research is not diversification, it is ignorance. He ran 1,400 at Magellan because he had the research capacity and because size forced it (*Beating the Street*, Ch. 4-6) — do not copy the count.
- **Diversify across categories, not for its own sake**: a mix of stalwarts, fast growers, some cyclicals/turnarounds/asset plays as the opportunities appear; he did not hold a fixed allocation. The more fast growers/turnarounds, the more upside and the more risk; stalwarts and slow growers stabilize.
- **Rotation**: the practice is to sell the stalwart that has made its 30-50% and buy another undervalued one; to move from a completed turnaround to a fresh one; from a peaking cyclical to a troughed one. Rotation is between *companies*, not in and out of the market.
- **Concentration in your edge**: the more you know a company, the larger it can be; the ones you know least should be smallest.
- **Cash**: hold cash when you cannot find companies that pass the story + price test; do not hold cash because you think the market is high (Ch. 5). *Beating the Street*, Ch. 1-3: over the long run, stocks beat bonds/cash; being fully invested in good companies is his default, and cash is the residual.
- **Avoid options, futures, and shorts** (Ch. 19): the leverage and time decay make them a bet on price, not on the company.

## Specific claims / data
- *Beating the Street*, Ch. 1: he lists his "25 Golden Rules" — among them: know what you own and why you own it; invest in companies not the market; the amateur can beat the professional by ignoring the herd; owning stocks is like having children — don't have more than you can handle; there is always something to worry about; nobody can predict rates, the economy, or the market; the best stock to buy may be the one you already own.
- *One Up*, Ch. 16: "in small portfolios I'd be comfortable owning between three and ten stocks" (paraphrase of his stated range).
- *One Up*, Ch. 17: buying in the October-December tax-selling window and in market crashes (he cites 1987) as his two best periodic opportunities.

## How to APPLY (decision rules)
1. Every holding gets a category and a written sell trigger at purchase (from `02`). Review them at each earnings date.
2. A sell recommendation must quote the story change or metric breach — never "up X%" / "down Y%".
3. Portfolio review: count holdings the user can genuinely follow; if the book exceeds ~10 names for a part-time investor, flag it as a following-capacity problem, not a diversification benefit.
4. Rotation suggestions must be same-category swaps or category-appropriate: stalwart → cheaper stalwart; completed turnaround → new turnaround/cyclical at trough.
5. If nothing passes, say "cash is the residual — no market call implied".
6. Refuse options/futures/shorting suggestions under this lens.

## Caveats / where he hedges
- The 3-10 range is for individuals with limited time; a full-time analyst team can follow more. It is a *follow-capacity* rule, not a risk-model output — position sizing and correlation are the risk seat's job.
- "Stocks beat bonds" and "stay invested" reflect the 1982-1993 experience; he acknowledges (Ch. 5, *Beating the Street* Ch. 2) that big drawdowns happen and that you must be able to hold through them financially and emotionally — money needed in the next few years does not belong in stocks.
- Rotation on a 30-50% stalwart gain is a heuristic, not a tax-aware rule; taxable accounts change the arithmetic.
