# Worldview — Invest in What You Know, Ignore the Market
> Source: *One Up on Wall Street* (Lynch & Rothchild, 1989; Millennium ed. 2000): Introduction, Part I (Ch. 1-5); *Beating the Street* (1993): Introduction ("Escape from Bondage"), Ch. 1 "The Miracle of St. Agnes", Ch. 2 "The Weekend Worrier", Ch. 3 "A Tour of the Fund House". Distilled 2026-09-22. Paraphrases with chapter pointers; no verbatim quotes beyond well-known short phrases.

## Core thesis
The individual investor can beat professionals because (a) they encounter products, stores and industries first-hand before Wall Street covers them, and (b) they are free of the institutional constraints — committees, career risk, the need to own what everyone else owns — that make professionals late and herd-like (*One Up*, Ch. 2 "The Wall Street Oxymorons"). The edge is only real if you use it: research the company, understand how it makes money, and hold through noise. Amateurs lose it by copying professionals, chasing hot tips, and trading on price moves.

## Key mental models
- **Invest in what you understand** (*One Up*, Ch. 3-4, 6). Personal/professional knowledge — your job, hobbies, shopping habits — is a legitimate research advantage. Knowing the product is the starting point, never the finishing point: you still do the numbers (Ch. 6 "Stalking the Tenbagger": "an amateur ... should be able to find..." — the point is that the sighting is a lead, not a buy).
- **The amateur's edge / Wall Street oxymorons** (Ch. 2). Professionals buy late (after a stock is "approved"), sell early, and prefer to fail conventionally. "Street lag": analysts cover a company after it has already run.
- **Tenbaggers hide in boring places** (Ch. 8 "The Perfect Stock, What a Deal!"). Attributes of the perfect stock: dull or ridiculous name, does something dull or disagreeable, a spinoff, institutions don't own it and analysts don't follow it, rumors of toxic waste/mafia, depressing industry, no-growth industry, a niche, a product people keep buying, technology *user* rather than maker, insiders buying, company buying back shares.
- **The stock market is a distraction from investing** (*One Up*, Ch. 5 "Is This a Good Market? Please Don't Ask"; *Beating the Street*, Ch. 2). Nobody can predict markets or interest rates; if you spend 13 minutes a year on economics you've wasted 10 (his often-repeated line, paraphrased). Corrections are recurring and unpredictable; the person who owns good companies through them wins.
- **The cocktail party theory** (*One Up*, Ch. 5). A sentiment gauge he offers half-seriously: when nobody talks stocks → near a bottom; when everyone tells *him* what to buy → near a top. He explicitly warns not to trade on it.
- **No technicals, no timing.** He does not use charts to decide; he does not sell because a stock is "too high" or buy because it is "down a lot" (Ch. 18 "The Twelve Silliest (and Most Dangerous) Things People Say About Stock Prices").
- **Diworsification** (Ch. 9 "Stocks I'd Avoid" and Ch. 8). Companies that use cash to buy unrelated businesses they don't understand destroy value; prefer buybacks or dividends to acquisitions outside the core.
- **Flowers and weeds** (*One Up*, Ch. 17; *Beating the Street*, Ch. 1 / 25 Golden Rules). Selling winners and holding losers is cutting the flowers and watering the weeds. Let a winner run while the story holds; sell the loser whose story broke.

## Specific claims, mechanisms & data (from the books)
- Magellan Fund 1977-1990: ~29% annualized; assets grew from ~$18M to ~$14B; he owned at times 1,400+ stocks (*Beating the Street*, Ch. 4-6) — the point being "as many as you can follow", not a fixed count.
- Examples he cites of amateur-edge finds: Hanes/L'eggs (his wife), Dunkin' Donuts, La Quinta, Taco Bell, Chrysler (turnaround, 1982), Fannie Mae (his biggest winner, *Beating the Street*, Ch. 18), Pep Boys, Stop & Shop.
- Losing examples he owns up to: Bildner's, Texas Air, First Executive; he stresses he was wrong roughly 4 in 10 times and that a few tenbaggers pay for many losers (*One Up*, Ch. 16-17).

## How to APPLY (decision rules for an agent using this lens)
1. Ask first: can the user (or the analyst) explain in plain words how the company makes money and why earnings will be higher in 3-5 years? If no → outside the edge, pass.
2. Treat a product sighting or personal-knowledge lead as a **lead**, not a thesis. Route to `04-metrics-and-thresholds.md`.
3. Score the "perfect stock" attributes (boring, ignored, low institutional ownership, insiders buying, buybacks, niche). More hits = more interesting, not automatically a buy.
4. Refuse to make or use market-level forecasts, interest-rate calls, or charts in the verdict. If asked "is the market too high?", answer: Lynch's lens is silent; judge the company.
5. Check for diworsification in the last 3 years of acquisitions; penalize unrelated deals.
6. Frame every sell as a **story change**, never as "up X%" / "down Y%".

## Caveats / where he hedges
- His edge era (1977-1990) predated internet disclosure and cheap screening; retail information asymmetry has shrunk. Buying "what you know" without doing the numbers is a well-documented way to lose (he says so himself, Ch. 6).
- He ran a huge, diversified, professionally supported fund — "as many stocks as you can follow" meant a research staff for him; for an amateur it means a handful.
- Boring-company tenbaggers were plentiful in a mid-cap-rich, less-efficient market; the base rate may be lower today.
- Ignoring macro worked in a secular bull (1982-2000). He admits (Ch. 5) he did not see 1987 coming and that it did not matter because his companies were fine — that is the argument, not "macro is never relevant".
